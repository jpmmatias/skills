Lesson 2 · Size the noise

Expected value and variance

A dashboard number wobbles from day to day. The expected value says where it is centred; the variance says how far it wobbles. Both can be worked out before a single user arrives.

Why this matters for your mission: a conversion rate is an average, and the standard error of an average tells you how big a day-to-day or arm-to-arm difference pure noise produces, which is exactly what you need before calling an A/B result.

How this page is grounded: every formula is derived here from the PMF of lesson 1, every number was recomputed by a script while writing the page, and the simulation below checks the central result, σ/√n, in front of you. The books under Go deeper could not be opened while this example was written, so they are marked unverified.

Five words first

E[X], μExpected value: the PMF-weighted average of X, where long-run averages settle.
Var(X), σ²Variance: the expected squared distance of X from μ.
σStandard deviation: √Var(X), in the same units as X.
independentKnowing one variable’s value tells you nothing about the other’s.
SEStandard error: the standard deviation of an estimate, such as a sample mean.

Expected value: the centre of a PMF

Weight every value by its probability and add:

E[X] = Σx x · P(X = x)

It is the average you would see over very many draws: by lesson 1’s experiment, value x makes up a share P(X = x) of a long run, so the run’s average heads to Σ x · P(X = x). The variance measures spread around it, and has a shortcut that is easier to compute:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ²  ·  σ = √Var(X)

  3. Var(X) = 91/6 − 3.5² = 35/12 ≈ 2.917, so σ = √(35/12) ≈ 1.708.

Rules for sums

Metrics are usually sums: revenue is the sum over orders, a conversion count is the sum of 0/1 outcomes. Two rules carry you through, and the difference between them matters.

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]   always
E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + b  ·  Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X)
Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)   only when X and Y are independent

Expectation is linear with no conditions, because E[X + Y] is a weighted sum over pairs of values and the sum splits into the X part and the Y part. Variance needs independence. Two dice show why:

Independent

Two separate dice, X and Y. The sum has E = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7 and Var = 35/12 + 35/12 = 35/6 ≈ 5.83 (σ ≈ 2.42). Listing all 36 equally likely pairs gives the same 35/6.

Dependent

One die X and Y = 7 − X, its opposite face. E[X + Y] = 7 still holds. But X + Y is always exactly 7, so Var(X + Y) = 0, not 5.83: the rule for variances fails.

Why does this line exist? Why does the variance rule carry the condition “only when independent” while the expectation rule does not?

Show the reason

Expanding the square gives Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X, Y), where the covariance Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] measures whether the two move together. Independence makes it 0. For Y = 7 − X it is −35/12, which exactly cancels the two variances. Expectation has no square, so no cross term ever appears.

Why averages wobble less: σ/√n

Take n independent draws X1, …, Xn, each with mean μ and standard deviation σ, and average them: X̄ = (X1 + … + Xn) / n. Apply the rules:

  1. Linearity: E[X1 + … + Xn] = nμ, so E[X̄] = nμ / n = μ. The average is centred on the truth.
  2. Independence: Var(X1 + … + Xn) = nσ².
  3. Dividing by n divides the variance by n²: Var(X̄) = nσ² / n² = σ²/n.

SE(X̄) = √(σ²/n) = σ / √n

The spread of an average shrinks with the square root of the sample size. Averaging 25 die rolls gives SE = 1.708 / 5 ≈ 0.342; averaging 100 gives 1.708 / 10 ≈ 0.171. Four times the data buys half the noise.

Experiment: watch the means narrow

Each of 1,000 simulated experiments rolls a fair die n times and records the mean. Bars show where the 1,000 means landed; the dashed line is the exact distribution of the mean of n dice (computed, not simulated); the bracket marks 3.5 ± σ/√n. This is a simulation with a seeded generator, so each seed is reproducible.

Predict first: going from n = 4 to n = 16, does the bracket get half as wide, a quarter as wide, or stay the same? Then move the slider. Compare the readout’s “standard deviation” of the simulated means with the formula’s σ/√n at every n, and press New seed to see how much that agreement depends on luck.

What you should see: the bracket halves from 0.85 to 0.43, the simulated standard deviation stays within a few percent of σ/√n at every n, and as n grows the histogram turns into a narrow bell (checked while writing: with seeds 7, 8 and 9, the simulated standard deviations were within 6% of σ/√n at every n). The bell shape is the central limit theorem, which the textbooks below prove1; this lesson needs only the σ/√n you derived. For a bell, about two-thirds of the means fall inside the bracket; the readout counts the actual share.

Back to your dashboard: a conversion rate is a mean

A conversion rate over n visitors is the mean of n 0/1 outcomes, so everything above applies with σ = √(p(1 − p)). Statistics texts call this the standard error of a sample proportion2:

SE = √(p(1 − p) / n)

With an illustrative 10% conversion rate (σ = 0.3):

visitors n√nSE = 0.3 / √nin percentage points

So a day with 2,500 visitors that reads 10.5% instead of 10% has moved by less than one standard error: that is ordinary noise. To halve the noise, from 0.6 to 0.3 points, you need 4 × 2,500 = 10,000 visitors.

Check yourself

Milestone challenge · Size the noise

Predict the wobble of one of your metrics

Pick a daily metric from your own dashboard that is an average or a rate (conversion rate, average order value, error rate). From one typical day of raw per-user data, compute σ (for a rate, √(p(1 − p))), n and the standard error σ/√n. Then take the metric’s daily values over at least 14 comparable days and compute their standard deviation. If you cannot share real data, simulate 14 days with your lesson 1 sampler and say so.

What to send back

The metric’s name and definition; n, σ and the SE for one day, with the arithmetic or the script; the list of daily values and their standard deviation; your answer on how many users halve the SE.

Pass criteria

  1. n, σ (with how it was computed) and SE = σ/√n are reported for one day, and the arithmetic reproduces the SE to 2 significant figures.
  2. The standard deviation of at least 14 daily values is reported next to the SE, with one sentence saying which is larger and by what factor.
  3. The number of users needed to halve the SE is stated and equals 4 × the day’s n.

When you are done, send your work to your teacher in the terminal.
